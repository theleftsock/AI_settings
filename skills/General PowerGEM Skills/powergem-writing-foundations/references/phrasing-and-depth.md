# Part 4. Phrasing and depth

Parts 1–3 teach discipline: what to cut, what to avoid. Discipline alone produces clean prose, not alive prose. This part is about the second thing: finding the phrasing that carries meaning rather than just delivering it. Studied from writers who have done this at the highest level.

## The underlying problem

A language model's default output is the most probable next word. Good prose often takes the second-most-probable word, the one that was almost the cliché but wasn't. The distance between those two words is where voice lives. Every principle below is a way of pushing writing off the most-predictable path toward the more specific, more alive one.

## Open on a concrete moment, not a category

Compare two openings:

> "In today's increasingly complex grid landscape, utilities face unprecedented challenges in resource planning."

> "At 2:47 a.m. on a Tuesday in February, a dispatcher in Dallas has to decide whether to fire up Unit 3."

The first names a category. The second names a moment. Category openings produce category writing, and readers stop tracking. Moment openings force the writer to know something specific, and that specificity is what earns attention.

Margaret Atwood's *The Blind Assassin* opens: "Ten days after the war ended, my sister Laura drove a car off a bridge." Amor Towles opens *A Gentleman in Moscow*: "At half past six on the twenty-first of June 1922..." Jesmyn Ward opens *Sing, Unburied, Sing*: "I like to think I know what death is." None of them warm up. The first sentence is already doing work.

**Rule:** Before writing any opening, name the specific moment, person, or fact the sentence is about. Start there.

## State the intent, then complicate it

Hanif Abdurraqib writes: "Here is where I would like to tell you about the form on my father's jump shot. The truth, though, is that I saw my father shoot a basketball only one time."

The move is to announce what you mean to say, then admit what's actually true. This is the opposite of the AI pattern of stating a thesis and immediately confirming it in the predicted way. It signals honesty. The writer is thinking, not performing.

Business-writing version: "The easy answer is that we're faster. The honest answer is that speed only matters if you trust the numbers underneath it, which is what we've actually been working on."

## Use bodily, physical metaphor for abstract things

Jesmyn Ward: "Sorrow is food swallowed too quickly, caught in the throat, making it nearly impossible to breathe."

Abstract nouns (reliability, efficiency, risk) are where writing dies. Readers can't picture them. The repair is to turn the abstraction into a physical action or object:

- Instead of "operational risk": "the phone call at 3 a.m."
- Instead of "forecasting accuracy": "the difference between committing a unit that pays and one that doesn't"
- Instead of "scalability": "what happens when your team grows from six analysts to sixty"

Bodies, rooms, tools, weather. Anything the reader can see.

## Sensory, tactile specificity builds trust

Anthony Doerr writes the shells as "the sleek egg of its body, the toothy gap of its aperture." You learn a page later that the character is blind, which means this precision is the only world he knows. The detail is the characterization.

Business application: the salesperson who can describe the actual dashboard an operator stares at, the exact moment in the quarterly cycle when the question comes up, the specific report a CFO flips to first, that salesperson is trusted. Generic claims ("improves decision-making") are not.

**Rule:** For every claim, ask: What is the physical object, time, or action this is actually about? Replace the claim with the object.

## Vary register deliberately: mix high and low

Cormac McCarthy pairs the stripped imperative ("See the child.") with baroque, nearly-Biblical sentences that run for half a page. Ferdia Lennon writes ancient Syracuse in modern Irish vernacular, complete with swearing, and it works because the collision is the point.

In business writing this translates to crisp, almost curt declarations sitting next to longer exploratory sentences. The shift signals that the writer is paying attention. Uniform register reads as mechanical.

A proposal paragraph shouldn't sound exactly like the paragraph before it.

## Let form serve content

Daniel Mason builds *North Woods* out of letters, case notes, medical records, poems, and pastiche. Each form carries something prose wouldn't. In business writing, the equivalent is knowing when to stop writing paragraphs and switch to a table, a Q&A, a timeline, a single bolded line, or a list with teeth. A pricing objection handled as a three-line exchange ("They'll ask: X. The honest answer: Y.") lands harder than a paragraph of context.

## Address the reader, but only when the message earns it

Abdurraqib: "You are putting your hand into my open palm..." He uses "you" because the book is a conversation. AI writing scatters "you" reflexively, to feel friendly. That reads as fake. Reserve direct address for moments when the writing is actually talking to the reader and not performing friendliness.

## Withhold context. Let detail precede explanation.

Doerr doesn't tell you the shell collector is blind. You feel your way into it through the tactile prose. Business writing over-explains. The instinct is to front-load: "As you know, PowerGEM is a leading provider of..." The stronger move is to start with a specific detail that implies the context, and let the reader catch up.

## The short sentence / long sentence rhythm

Every author on this list does this. Ward, Atwood, McCarthy, Towles. A long, exploratory sentence that builds an image or an argument through subordinate clauses, letting the thought breathe and turn and find its own end. Then: a short one. The short one lands.

Never write three sentences in a row of similar length. Feel the rhythm as you write. If it's metronomic, break it.

## The second phrasing test

After writing any sentence of real consequence (the opening, a pivot, a claim), ask: Was this the first phrasing that came to mind? If yes, it's almost certainly the most-probable output. Find the second phrasing. Often the second phrasing isn't fancier. It's more specific. The first said "operators use our tool." The second says "a planner spends Tuesday afternoons running cases in our tool."

This single habit is the difference between depth and default.

## The specificity ladder

When a sentence feels generic, walk down the ladder:

1. Category ("customers")
2. Segment ("utility planning teams")
3. Role ("the VP of resource planning")
4. Person or scene ("a VP in Houston, on a Thursday call with her board, trying to defend a capacity number")

Stop at the lowest rung you can honestly support. Most business writing stops at rung 1 or 2. Good writing usually lives at 3 or 4.

## What this is not

These principles are not a license for purple prose in a sales email. Nobody wants a cold-outreach message that opens like a McCarthy novel. The point is that even a four-sentence email benefits from a specific opening, a physical image somewhere in it, at least one sentence with a rhythm different from the others, and a close that says something only we would say. Those moves are small and nearly invisible. They are the difference between a message that gets read and one that gets deleted.

## Sources studied for Part 4

- Hanif Abdurraqib, *There's Always This Year: On Basketball and Ascension* (2024). Direct address, intent-then-undercut, lyrical confession.
- Daniel Mason, *North Woods* (2023). Form shifts, pastiche, documentary registers.
- Ferdia Lennon, *Glorious Exploits* (2024). Register collision, vernacular in formal settings.
- Jesmyn Ward, *Sing, Unburied, Sing* (2017). Bodily metaphor for abstraction.
- Amor Towles, *A Gentleman in Moscow* (2016). Precision of time and word choice.
- Margaret Atwood, *The Blind Assassin* (2000). Fact-forward opening.
- Cormac McCarthy, *Blood Meridian* (1985). Register extremes, imperative prose.
- Anthony Doerr, *The Shell Collector* (2002). Sensory specificity, withheld context.
