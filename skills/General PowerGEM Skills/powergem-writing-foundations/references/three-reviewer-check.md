# Part 6. The three-reviewer check

After a piece is drafted and the Part 3 ship-check has been run, Part 6 runs a separate pass. Where Part 3 is the writer's self-review for craft, Part 6 imagines three different readers with three different jobs. They disagree on purpose. The gaps between their scores are where the real problems live.

## How it works

Each reviewer scores the piece out of 10 against their own criteria. The three scores appear together in a short block under the draft, with one sentence per reviewer explaining what drove the number. Any score below 6 halts the process: the criteria that pulled it down are named, and the writer is asked whether to revise before shipping. Scores of 6 or 7 get a flagged note but not a full stop. Eights and above ship clean.

Scoring is integer, not decimal. Decimals invite false precision. A 7 and an 8 should feel meaningfully different.

## Reviewer 1: Senior copywriter

A craft-first editor who has read too much mediocre B2B writing and knows the tells. This reviewer does not care about the sale. Only the prose.

**Criteria:**

1. Zero em dashes.
2. No words or phrases from the Part 2 deny-list.
3. Opening names a specific moment, person, or fact rather than a category.
4. Sentence rhythm varies. Never three similar-length sentences in a row.
5. At least one sentence of consequence passes the second-phrasing test.
6. Claims sit at rung 3 or 4 of the specificity ladder where the content allows.
7. Closing does real work, not a restated summary.
8. Parallel construction in every list, series, or comparison.
9. Active voice is the default. Passive only where it earns its place.
10. The piece could not lose another 20% without losing meaning.

## Reviewer 2: Two members of the target audience

The two readers the piece is aimed at. They are named explicitly before scoring so the writer can push back if the personas do not fit. For an MCast LinkedIn post: a load-forecasting analyst and a VP of resource planning. For a CEO-facing one-pager: a utility CEO and a CFO. The score is the average of the two. When the two readers would diverge, the divergence gets called out in the note.

**Criteria:**

1. The opening sounds like it already knows my job.
2. The technical description of the product is accurate to how I would describe it myself.
3. The numbers cited are from sources I would consider credible.
4. The writer has clearly spent time with someone who does what I do.
5. I'm not being talked down to or talked at.
6. The piece respects the time I have to read it.
7. The call to action is clear and low-effort.
8. I recognize something specific from my own week.
9. I would consider forwarding this to a colleague.
10. I would consider replying.

## Reviewer 3: PowerGEM marketing executive

A VP of marketing at PowerGEM who has to answer to the CEO for whatever ships. Cares about craft and audience fit, but weighs them against brand integrity, legal exposure, and whether the piece moves a business objective forward.

**Criteria:**

1. Voice, terminology, and register match PowerGEM brand standards.
2. Every claim is defensible, with sources cited or available.
3. The piece points to the correct product, for the correct audience, at the correct funnel stage.
4. PowerGEM is differentiated, not interchangeable with competitors.
5. The call to action maps to a next step our funnel actually supports.
6. Format fits the channel. A LinkedIn post reads as a LinkedIn post, not a shortened blog.
7. Any competitor references are handled correctly, or absent.
8. Quote and data attribution meet the Part 5 source fidelity standard.
9. No regulatory, legal, or disclosure risk introduced.
10. The CEO would be comfortable sharing this under their own name.

## How the output looks

After a clean draft and a clean Part 3 ship-check, the report reads:

> **Three-reviewer check**
> Copywriter: 8/10. Strong rhythm and specificity. One hedge could still be cut in the product paragraph.
> Audience (load-forecasting analyst + VP resource planning): 7/10 average. The analyst rated it 8. The VP rated it 6 because the stakes paragraph spoke more to an operator than a planner.
> Marketing executive: 9/10. On brand, on funnel, defensible.
>
> No scores below 6. Cleared to ship unless you want to tighten the VP-facing paragraph.

When a score lands below 6, the report reads:

> **Three-reviewer check**
> Audience (utility CEO): 5/10. The opening assumes familiarity with day-ahead market timing that a CEO would not have. The product paragraph is fine. The CTA is fine. The gap is the opening.
>
> Would you like to revise it, or ship as is and accept the risk?

## Design choices worth flagging

The audience reviewer is two readers, not one, because a single composite prospect collapses into the customer we already have in our heads. Two named readers from different seats force real tension.

The marketing executive scores last, not first, on purpose. Running brand last prevents pre-sanitizing the copy into safe blandness before the copywriter and audience reviewers weigh in.
