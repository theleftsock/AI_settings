# Part 5. Source fidelity

The work of sales writing is usually to put things into our own words. This part is the opposite. When we bring in a customer's voice, a speaker on a panel, a data point from a report, or a quote from a webinar transcript, the material is not ours to rework. Source fidelity is the discipline for handling it.

## Keep quotes verbatim

What a person said is what we print. Grammar, pauses, small repetitions, and all. If Sarah Graupman said "we will give back what is the demand on that hourly," we do not clean it up to "we report hourly demand." The quote goes in as she said it.

Two narrow exceptions apply.

First, obvious transcription errors of proper nouns get corrected silently. A transcript that reads "EMCAST" becomes "MCast." "DZayer" becomes "DAYZER." "PowerGym" becomes "PowerGEM." "I-SO New England" becomes "ISO New England." The speaker said the real name. The machine mis-heard it. This is hygiene, not editing.

Second, a single stutter or false start of one word can be removed silently when it does not change meaning. "We, we've been working on that" becomes "we've been working on that." Anything beyond one word requires an ellipsis or it stops being the quote.

## Truncation with [...]

The square-bracket ellipsis is standard and welcome when a quoted passage runs too long. Two tests govern its use. The shortened version must remain true to the speaker's original intent. And the device cannot be used so often that the quote reads as a collage. A working rule: no more than one [...] per quoted passage in external content. Two at the absolute limit.

## Never stitch moments

If a speaker said something at 12:03 of a transcript and something else at 33:15, those are two quotes. Introduce them separately. Stitching two moments into one continuous-looking passage, even when every word is accurate, is a fabricated quote.

## Brackets for clarification only

Square brackets inserted inside a quote carry the minimum a reader needs to make sense of the passage. A pronoun replaced with a name. A noun added for context. "[The dispatcher] has to decide" is fine. Brackets never carry tone or color. "That was [amazing]" is not a thing we do.

## Keep hedges

If the speaker said "I think," "probably," or "as far as I know," the hedge stays. Removing a hedge makes a speaker sound more certain than they were, which is unfair to them and exposes PowerGEM when the claim turns out to be softer than we said it was.

## Paraphrase lives outside quotes

Once a sentence is inside quotation marks, it is verbatim. If the goal is to summarize someone's point in your own words, do so without quotes and with attribution. Sarah described the forecast as hourly, fifteen days out, delivered through flat files, API, or web interface. That is a paraphrase. It should not be confused with a quote.

## Consent for named customers

Internal material is flexible. Public-facing content that ties a quote to a named person at a specific customer is not. The customer approves the wording and the context before it ships. That covers email, LinkedIn, case studies, landing pages, decks going outside the company, and webinars.

## Cite well enough to verify

For a transcript: speaker, event name, date, and either a timestamp or a passage long enough for someone to find. For data: the primary report, the issuing body, the year, and a page or section if the claim is specific. Not "according to some sources." Not "studies show."

## Primary over secondary

EIA's *Short-Term Energy Outlook* is a primary source. An industry newsletter summarizing it is a secondary source. For external content we quote the primary. A secondary source is where we found the thing. A primary source is what the thing actually says.

## Do not cherry-pick

A quote in isolation should not mean something different than the quote in context. If pulling seven words from a customer makes her sound like she's endorsing a claim she actually qualified heavily, pick a different quote or include the qualification.

## Case-study integrity

Composite characters work in thought-leadership content when we flag them as illustrative. A case study tied to a real customer logo contains only verifiable facts, and the customer has approved the whole of it.

## Numbers that drift

A number cited in a secondary source, rounded, then rounded again, is not the number anymore. Go back to the original every time. 75.8 GW does not become "almost 80 GW" in our content.

## Quoting ourselves

Past PowerGEM content is not current PowerGEM content. When we quote our own earlier webinar, whitepaper, or blog post, the same rules apply. Verbatim, attributed to the original context, truncatable only under the ellipsis rule above.
