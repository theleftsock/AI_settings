# PowerGEM Writing Foundations

A foundation layer for every piece of written content PowerGEM produces. The brand voice tells us how we sound. These foundations tell us how good writing works.

Drawn from Strunk & White's *The Elements of Style*, Garner's *Modern English Usage*, and current research on the patterns that give AI-written content away.

## How to use this skill

For any writing task, apply Parts 1, 2, and 3 below as the default discipline. They cover the rules that matter most and the patterns most likely to make prose read as AI-generated.

For work of real consequence (public-facing copy, long-form content, anything going to a customer under PowerGEM's name), also read the reference files:

- `references/phrasing-and-depth.md`: Part 4. How to push phrasing off the most-predictable path. Read when drafting openings, pivots, and claims that need to carry weight.
- `references/source-fidelity.md`: Part 5. Rules for quotes, data, and attribution. Read whenever a customer quote, speaker transcript, or cited data point is involved.
- `references/three-reviewer-check.md`: Part 6. A structured pre-ship review with three distinct reviewers. Run on anything going outside the company.

The quick self-check in Part 3 is a six-question pass to run on every draft before delivering it.

## Part 1. Principles of good writing

### Cut ruthlessly

If a sentence still works without a word, delete the word. Replace wordy constructions with shorter equivalents:

- "in order to" becomes "to"
- "at this point in time" becomes "now"
- "due to the fact that" becomes "because"
- "the fact that" is usually deletable
- "a large number of" becomes "many"
- "make a decision" becomes "decide"
- "prior to" becomes "before"
- "utilize" becomes "use"
- "facilitate" becomes "help"
- "with respect to" becomes "about"

Short, plain words beat long ones when the meaning is equivalent.

### Prefer the active voice

"The team built the model" beats "The model was built by the team." Passive is acceptable when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or the action itself is the point. Never default to passive to soften a message.

### Write with nouns and verbs, not adjectives and adverbs

Strong verbs carry meaning. Piling on modifiers signals a weak verb. "The analysis revealed three risks" beats "The very comprehensive analysis carefully revealed three major risks."

Avoid vague intensifiers entirely: very, really, quite, rather, somewhat, pretty much. They add no information.

### Be specific, concrete, and definite

Name the thing. "Our production cost model" beats "our solution." "$2.3M in annual savings" beats "significant savings." Specific claims build credibility. Vague claims erode it.

### Put statements in positive form

"She usually arrived late" beats "She was not often on time." Use negation when the negation itself is the point, not as a hedge.

### Use parallel construction for parallel ideas

"We build, test, and deploy," not "We build, test, and are deploying." Lists, series, and comparisons follow the same grammatical pattern.

### Keep related words together

Modifiers belong next to what they modify. Misplaced modifiers create ambiguity or unintended meaning.

### Don't overwrite or overstate

Avoid exclamation points, superlatives, and hype. Trusted experts do not shout. Replace marketing clichés with specific, evidence-backed claims:

- "industry-leading": name the metric and the ranking
- "best-in-class": name the class and the comparison
- "revolutionary" or "game-changing": describe what changed, for whom
- "cutting-edge": describe the capability plainly

### Usage discipline (Garner)

Use precise words precisely:

- *comprise* does not equal *compose*. The whole comprises the parts. The parts compose the whole.
- *fewer* counts discrete items. *Less* measures uncountable quantity.
- *affect* is the verb. *Effect* is usually the noun.
- *impact* is a noun. Prefer *affect* or *influence* as the verb in formal writing.
- *that* restricts. *Which* adds non-essential information (with a comma).

Technical energy-industry terms (production cost modeling, LMP forecasting, resource adequacy, DA/RT markets, capacity expansion) are expected and welcome. They signal expertise. Generic business buzzwords (synergy, leverage as a verb, optimize, unlock, empower, foster) are not.

Avoid legalese and bureaucratese: pursuant to, heretofore, in the event that, hereunder.

### Contractions

Use sparingly. They warm the tone in email and outreach. They feel out of place in proposals, RFP responses, and formal decks.

### Sentence rhythm

Vary length. Long sentences explain. Short sentences land. A paragraph of uniform sentences feels mechanical. Start sentences with the subject when possible. Front-loading subordinate clauses ("Given the complexity of...") slows the reader.

### Revise

First drafts are not final drafts. On the second pass, cut 20%. The best writing feels effortless because the effort is hidden in what was removed.

## Part 2. Things that give away AI writing

These are the patterns current readers have learned to spot. Treat this section as a deny-list.

### The em dash: a hard rule

Do not use em dashes in PowerGEM content. Not one per paragraph. Not one per page. Zero.

The em dash is the single most recognizable AI tell in 2026. Readers notice it immediately. Any sentence containing an em dash can be rewritten with a period, comma, colon, or set of parentheses. Always make the substitution.

Replacement guide:

- Em dash setting off an emphatic clause: use a period or colon. "We built one tool: a production cost model that closes faster than anything in the market."
- Em dash as a parenthetical aside: use commas or parentheses. "The model, running on our simulation engine, closes in under an hour."
- Em dash between two related sentences: use a period. "The model closes fast. That matters when RFP deadlines are tight."
- Em dash indicating a pause or shift: use a period followed by a short sentence. "The cost savings are real. They show up in the first quarter."

This rule applies to en dashes in the same role. Hyphens are fine for compound modifiers ("day-ahead market"). The em dash and the long en dash used as punctuation are out.

### Words to avoid

The canonical AI vocabulary. Do not use these in PowerGEM content unless the technical context truly requires them:

delve, tapestry, realm, landscape, leverage, harness, utilize, robust, pivotal, seamless, innovative, cutting-edge, multifaceted, comprehensive, streamline, optimize, empower, unlock, foster, navigate (as a metaphor), testament, underpinnings, furthermore, moreover, additionally, crucial, vital, essential, profound, remarkable, noteworthy, illuminate, underscore.

### Phrases to avoid

- "At its core..."
- "It's important to note..."
- "Delve into..."
- "Navigating the complexities of..."
- "In the realm of..."
- "A testament to..."
- "That being said..."
- "To put it simply..."
- "In today's fast-paced world..."
- "In the ever-evolving landscape of..."
- "Unlock the power of..."
- "Take your [X] to the next level"

### Sentence structures to avoid

**The "not X, but Y" construction.** AI relies on this constantly: "This isn't just a model, it's a framework for decision-making." Use it once if it earns its place. Never twice in the same document.

**Triadic lists everywhere.** AI defaults to the Rule of Three in every sentence: "fast, flexible, and reliable," "build, test, and deploy," "simple, scalable, and secure." Triads are fine when they reflect reality. They are tells when every list is a triad. Mix in pairs, fours, and single emphatic items.

**Semicolon overload.** Use semicolons when a true compound structure calls for one. Otherwise prefer a period.

**Uniform sentence length.** AI writes sentences of similar length, one after another, creating a metronomic rhythm. Break it on purpose. Short punch. Then a longer sentence that explores the idea with a subordinate clause or two. Then short again.

**Uniform paragraph structure.** AI paragraphs often follow the same internal recipe: topic sentence, three supporting points, concluding bow. Vary it. Some paragraphs should be one sentence. Some should end abruptly without a summary.

**The wrap-up-with-a-bow ending.** AI closes every section with a neat restatement. Don't. Let the last concrete point do the work.

**"Represents / signifies / stands for" framing.** AI loves telling you what things represent: "This tool represents a shift in how operators think about reliability." Just state the thing. "This tool changes how operators think about reliability."

**Excessive transitions.** Furthermore, Moreover, Additionally, However, Consequently piled up across paragraphs. Good prose needs fewer connective words than we think.

**Weak analogy followed by abandonment.** AI opens with "Think of X as a house..." and then doesn't extend the analogy usefully. If an analogy isn't doing real work, cut it.

### Tonal tells

**Relentless positivity.** AI avoids stating things plainly when they're negative or uncertain. Be willing to write: "This approach won't work for you if..." or "The tradeoff is real."

**Excessive hedging.** "It's worth considering that potentially, in some cases, this might..." State things directly. If a claim needs a caveat, give one specific caveat, not a cloud of them.

**Covering every angle.** AI wants to be comprehensive and balanced on every topic. Human writing picks a point of view and defends it. Good PowerGEM content commits.

**Stiff, formal openings.** "In an era of increasing grid complexity..." Humans open with specifics, characters, or direct statements. Start where the action is.

### Formatting tells

**Erratic bolding of random key phrases mid-paragraph.** Bold sparingly and for a reason.

**Bulleting prose that should be paragraphs.** Bullets are for genuinely parallel, discrete items. Explanation belongs in prose.

**Headers on everything.** Short documents don't need five headers. Let paragraphs carry the structure.

## Part 3. A quick self-check before anything ships

Before sending or publishing, read the piece through once with these six questions:

1. Could I cut 20% without losing meaning?
2. Does every sentence have the same rhythm, or does length vary on purpose?
3. Are the claims specific, or are they hedged and generic?
4. Am I using any of the words or phrases on the AI deny-list?
5. Does the ending earn its place, or is it just tying a bow?
6. Are there any em dashes? If so, replace every one.

If the answer to any of these is wrong, revise before shipping.

## When to go deeper

Parts 1 through 3 are the floor. For work that needs more than discipline (a cold email that has to land, a proposal that needs to stand out, a one-pager a CEO will actually read), open the references:

- **`references/phrasing-and-depth.md`** tells you how to push writing off the most-predictable path and find phrasing that carries meaning rather than just delivering it. Read this before drafting openings, product claims, or any sentence of real consequence.
- **`references/source-fidelity.md`** is mandatory whenever a quote, transcript, data point, or cited figure is involved. It covers verbatim handling, truncation rules, consent for named customers, and how to avoid the subtle misrepresentations that destroy trust.
- **`references/three-reviewer-check.md`** is the pre-ship review to run on anything going public. Three reviewers with different jobs score the piece and disagree on purpose. The gaps between scores are where the real problems live.

Use good judgment about which references to load. A three-line Slack message does not need a three-reviewer check. A landing page does.
